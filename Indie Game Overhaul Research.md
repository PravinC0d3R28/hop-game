# **Evidence-Based Overhaul Strategy for Procedural WebGL Arcade Games**

## **Executive Verdict**

The optimal strategic path for the overhaul of the mobile WebGL game "HOP" demands a strict synthesis of performance-conscious rendering and empirically validated player retention mechanics. The primary recommendation is the adoption of a "Neon/Synthwave" art direction paired with a continuous, mid-run world transition structure, governed by a meta-progression model based entirely on score milestones and in-run missions. This specific configuration satisfies the rigorous technical constraints of a sub-20KB footprint without external asset dependencies or post-processing overhead. The Neon/Synthwave aesthetic mathematically repurposes the existing procedural halftone shader into high-contrast geometric rendering, bypassing the heavy algorithmic costs associated with generating organic, layered environments typical of paper-craft styles. Furthermore, implementing mid-run phase transitions capitalizes on the psychological principles of the flow state, providing necessary cognitive rest phases that directly combat the steep Day 7 (D7) churn rates prevalent in hyper-casual mobile titles. By eschewing dual-currency economies and complex physics-based power-ups in favor of streak milestones and dynamic platform swaying, the game introduces deep mechanical variety and session goals without inflating the underlying architecture or risking garbage collection stutters during execution.

## **Art Direction: Precedents and Procedural Feasibility**

The visual aesthetic of a hyper-casual mobile game serves as the primary driver for initial user acquisition and Day 1 (D1) retention. In the context of a purely procedural WebGL environment constrained by strict file size and performance limits, the choice of art direction must balance visual impact against algorithmic complexity. Evaluating the proposed styles—Neon/Synthwave, Cosmic/Minimal Space, and Paper Craft/Storybook—reveals distinct correlations between rendering strategies, historical market success, and technical viability.  
The "Paper Craft/Storybook" aesthetic, famously utilized by titles such as *Alto's Adventure* and *Monument Valley*, commands immense critical acclaim and exceptional long-term player retention due to its relaxing, low-friction cognitive load1. This style relies heavily on warm, soft color palettes, dynamic sky gradients, and a pronounced sense of atmospheric depth achieved through multi-layered parallax scrolling4. However, executing this aesthetic well in a purely procedural environment presents severe technical hurdles. Achieving the volumetric softness, organic shapes, and textured paper grain necessary to prevent the art from looking primitive requires complex noise algorithms (such as Simplex or Perlin noise) calculated either on the CPU and uploaded to canvas textures, or calculated per-fragment in custom shaders5. Generating high-resolution noise textures dynamically at boot introduces significant initialization delays, while runtime shader noise severely impacts the strict 60fps target on mid-range mobile GPUs. *Alto's Adventure*, for instance, relies on custom lit shaders blending ambient light colors and multiple additive planes to achieve its weather and time-of-day transitions4. For a game restricted from downloading external assets like cloud sprites or pre-baked textures, synthesizing organic depth procedurally within a 20KB budget is a high-risk endeavor likely to yield sub-premium results.  
Conversely, the "Cosmic/Minimal Space" direction, seen in hyper-casual hits like Ketchapp's *Stack* or Voodoo's *Helix Jump*, represents the most computationally economical approach6. A cosmic theme requires little more than a dark clear color and simple point primitives for starfields, minimizing draw calls and texture memory. However, extreme minimalism carries significant retention risks. Without the atmospheric depth or color dynamism provided by distinct environments, the visual hierarchy flattens. Market data indicates that purely minimalist hyper-casual games suffer from aggressive drop-offs, with D7 retention often plunging below 8% and Day 30 (D30) retention struggling to break 3%7. The lack of visual variety fails to reward progression, making extended sessions feel monotonous. To succeed, minimal games must rely entirely on flawlessly tuned kinetic feedback and "juice," which places immense pressure on the core gameplay loop to sustain engagement independently.  
The "Neon/Synthwave" direction emerges as the most viable and structurally appropriate aesthetic for the "HOP" overhaul. Demonstrating a strong precedent in successful rhythm and arcade games like *Tiles Hop: EDM Rush*—which boasts over 500 million downloads—this high-contrast style inherently supports fast-paced, reaction-based gameplay8. From a procedural standpoint, Neon/Synthwave is mathematically pure and highly efficient. The baseline game already utilizes a custom MeshToonMaterial with a tri-planar halftone dot projection10. This infrastructure can be seamlessly modified; the dot calculations can be mathematically transformed into neon grid lines or scanlines using simple trigonometric functions, entirely avoiding the need for heavy noise generation. The stark contrast of glowing geometry against dark backgrounds naturally guides the player's eye to interactive elements, reducing visual clutter and cognitive fatigue. Furthermore, the vibrant, high-energy palette aligns with the empirical observation that games utilizing bright, neon art styles combined with clear color harmonies achieve highly consistent store presence and user engagement9. By leveraging mathematically generated shapes and high-contrast color theory, the Neon/Synthwave approach maximizes the perceived production value without taxing the mobile WebGL rendering pipeline.

## **Procedural-Art Quality: Techniques and Pitfalls**

Elevating procedural art from a basic, low-effort appearance to a premium aesthetic requires disciplined WebGL rendering strategies that bypass the need for heavy post-processing effects like true volumetric bloom or screen-space ambient occlusion. The distinction between a premium indie title and a rudimentary prototype often lies in the nuanced application of depth, color harmony, and material properties.  
A primary technique for establishing premium visual depth without asset dependencies is the implementation of procedural sky gradients. Premium games actively avoid flat, monochromatic backgrounds, which visually flatten the scene and diminish the perception of scale2. In a procedural WebGL context, a highly performant sky gradient can be generated by drawing a CanvasGradient onto a minimal 2D HTML canvas (e.g., ![][image1] pixels) during the game's boot sequence. This canvas is then instantiated as a THREE.CanvasTexture and mapped to a background plane or the scene's environment property11. Setting the texture's magnification and minification filters to THREE.LinearFilter ensures a smooth, interpolated transition of colors across the screen without banding artifacts13. This technique provides the illusion of infinite atmospheric depth at the cost of a single, minuscule texture allocation in GPU memory.  
Achieving a "fake glow" is essential for the Neon/Synthwave direction, particularly given the strict prohibition against post-processing passes like UnrealBloomPass, which would necessitate rendering the scene to multiple off-screen targets, downscaling, blurring, and compositing, thereby destroying the performance budget14. Premium procedural games simulate this effect through additive blending. By creating slightly oversized, camera-facing sprite planes positioned behind the primary platform meshes and applying a radial gradient CanvasTexture set to THREE.AdditiveBlending, the renderer mathematically adds the pixel values to the background colors13. This creates a convincing illusion of luminescence that scales perfectly across devices, requiring only a minimal increase in transparent draw calls while entirely bypassing the computational heft of fragment-based bloom thresholds.  
Palette discipline is another cornerstone of premium procedural generation. Generating random colors mathematically often results in jarring, visually offensive clashes. Premium titles constrain procedural color generation to specific harmonious rulesets, utilizing the Hue, Saturation, Lightness (HSL) color space rather than RGB. By fixing saturation and lightness to specific ranges (e.g., high saturation and lightness for neon accents, low lightness for backgrounds) and only mathematically iterating the hue value across a defined mathematical curve, the system guarantees analogous or complementary color harmonies15. This ensures that dynamically generated worlds always remain aesthetically pleasing, regardless of the randomization seed.  
A significant design choice in the baseline game that currently undermines its visual maturity is the reliance on thick, black outlines. The baseline architecture generates these outlines using the "inverted hull" technique, wherein every mesh is duplicated, scaled outward slightly, and rendered with a black MeshBasicMaterial10. While this successfully creates a comic-book aesthetic, it introduces a severe performance penalty by literally doubling the vertex count and the number of draw calls per frame. Removing these outlines is not merely a stylistic pivot toward a softer, more modern appearance; it is a critical performance optimization. Shedding this geometry overhead reclaims 50% of the rendering budget, freeing up processing cycles for dynamic features like moving platforms and expanded particle bursts. Premium indie games frequently avoid harsh outlines, relying instead on lighting, color contrast, and soft shadows to separate the foreground from the background, creating a more sophisticated and less cluttered visual experience.  
Developers relying on canvas-generated textures must also navigate severe WebGL performance pitfalls. A common and catastrophic mistake in procedural WebGL generation is updating CanvasTexture objects during the active render loop. Executing a command such as texture.needsUpdate \= true forces the browser to halt rendering, serialize the canvas pixel array, and upload it from system memory to the GPU's VRAM. On mobile devices, this operation causes severe main-thread blocking, manifesting as massive frame drops and visible stuttering17. To maintain a flawless 60fps target, all procedural canvas textures—including halftones, gradients, and UI elements—must be drawn, uploaded to the GPU, and finalized exclusively during the initial boot sequence or behind a loading screen during a world transition.

## **World Tiering and Pacing**

The structural pacing of an endless runner dictates the cadence of the player's cognitive load, which in turn directly influences session length and long-term retention. The overhaul must choose between structuring the game as a single continuous run with mid-run phase transitions, or separating the worlds into distinct, selectable levels via a meta-map.  
Empirical evidence strongly favors the implementation of mid-run world transitions for games strictly focused on a singular, hypnotic core loop. Continuous endless runs that shift visually and mechanically at specific score thresholds capitalize on the psychological concept of the "Flow State," a condition where a player's skill level perfectly aligns with the game's presented challenge19. If a game's difficulty scales linearly without interruption, the challenge eventually outpaces human reaction time, leading to inevitable failure and frustration21. Conversely, if the difficulty plateaus, the player becomes bored.  
By integrating mid-run transitions, the game introduces a "sawtooth" difficulty curve. As the player approaches a transition threshold, the difficulty peaks. Upon crossing the threshold—visually represented by a golden gate platform, an abrupt palette shift, and a burst of confetti—the raw numerical difficulty (such as game speed or platform spacing) drops slightly, offering a psychological "micro-rest" phase20. This moment of visual celebration combined with a temporary reduction in stress reinforces positive feedback loops, encouraging the player to continue the session to see the new environment.  
Contrasting this with a separate-run world selection model reveals significant retention friction. Map-based world selection forces a hard stop to the gameplay loop upon failure. The player is ejected to a menu screen, required to evaluate their progress, and physically elect to start a new level. In hyper-casual and arcade design, every interaction with a menu is a psychological exit opportunity24. For a game relying on rapid iteration and the "one more try" mentality, forcing players out of the active game state to navigate worlds disrupts the flow state and increases the likelihood of session abandonment.  
Industry benchmarks further contextualize this structural decision. In 2024, mobile gaming benchmarks revealed that the median D1 retention across all mobile games was approximately 22%, with the top 25% of games achieving 31% to 33%7. However, D7 retention plummets to roughly 8%, and D30 retention often falls below 3% for casual titles7. Hyper-casual titles are particularly vulnerable to this steep drop-off because they often lack the depth required to sustain long-term engagement. The evolution into "hybrid-casual" game design attempts to solve this by taking hyper-casual core mechanics and layering them with deeper progression systems that extend individual playtime sessions26. Games that successfully string together continuous loops with varied environmental phases inherently lengthen the average session playtime. Because higher daily playtime correlates strongly with better user acquisition ROI and overall lifetime value (LTV), extending the immediate session through seamless mid-run transitions is the most effective structural strategy for the "HOP" overhaul28.

## **Meta-Progression and Retention Mechanics**

Integrating meta-progression into a tiny arcade game requires identifying mechanics that drive maximum psychological engagement with a minimal footprint in the codebase. The options presented in the brainstorm—stars/dual currencies, per-world missions, streak milestones, and rare power-ups—each carry distinct technical and behavioral implications.  
The introduction of a dual-currency system (e.g., collecting "Stars" to unlock worlds while keeping "Coins" for skins) is a standard retention driver in mid-core games, designed to separate cosmetic progression from mechanical progression29. However, within the context of a lightweight web game, this introduces prohibitive overhead. A secondary currency necessitates the creation of new user interface elements, dedicated persistence logic for sanitization and storage, and runtime physics calculations for spawning and collecting a new type of entity alongside existing gems30. Furthermore, dual economies require extensive balancing to prevent inflation or progression stalling; if a player has enough coins but lacks stars, they experience artificial friction that can lead to churn29. Given the strict sub-20KB size constraint, the architectural bloat required to execute a dual currency flawlessly outweighs its benefits.  
Conversely, utilizing score milestones to drive progression is phenomenally efficient. The baseline game's architecture already continuously calculates the runtime variable dt.score and persists the highest value to localStorage via Zt.bestScore10. Tying meta-progression to this existing metric requires virtually zero new architecture; it only requires a configuration array mapping score thresholds to unlockable themes or starting gates. This approach leverages the psychological "curiosity gap"—the innate human desire to discover what lies beyond the next milestone31. By clearly displaying the next unlock threshold on the game-over screen, the game establishes an immediate session goal, transforming a generic high-score chase into a targeted pursuit of novel content.  
Streak milestones (Feature F5) and per-world missions (Feature F3) operate on the principle of micro-engagement. While macro-progression (unlocking World 3\) might take weeks, players require immediate, short-term positive reinforcement to remain engaged during a single session. The baseline game already tracks dt.perfectStreak for scoring logic10. By monitoring this variable and triggering a localized UI banner and a coin multiplier whenever the streak reaches intervals of five, the game provides localized dopamine hits. This validates the player's mastery of the game's mechanics in real-time. Similarly, simple in-run missions (e.g., "Collect 10 Gems in one run") provide players with alternative objectives. If a player is struggling to beat their high score, they can instead focus on completing a mission to earn coins, ensuring that every session feels rewarding and mitigates the frustration of a failed run.  
The inclusion of rare physics-based power-ups, such as a Magnet (Feature F6), presents a deceptive technical cost. While a magnet power-up is empirically proven to be a highly satisfying mechanic in endless runners, its implementation is computationally expensive30. Attracting gems toward the player's ball requires running per-frame distance checks against all active gem entities, calculating normalized directional vectors, and applying interpolation algorithms to simulate magnetic acceleration. Executing these calculations continuously within a JavaScript physics loop, while simultaneously maintaining a locked 60fps on low-end mobile devices, introduces unnecessary risk. The return on investment for this feature is poor when compared to passive, state-driven rewards like streak milestones, which require only a single integer evaluation.

## **Difficulty Curves and Game Feel**

The tuning of a game's difficulty curve directly dictates its "Game Feel," a concept extensively documented by researcher Steve Swink. Game feel is defined by the real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, where interactions are emphasized by polish and feedback19. The baseline version of "HOP" relies on a linear ramp formula for difficulty, where variables like platform spacing and jump duration scale linearly with the score until they hit a predefined hard cap10.  
While mathematically simple, linear difficulty scaling is fundamentally flawed for long-term retention. A linear curve steadily increases the cognitive and physical demands on the player until it inevitably surpasses human processing limits, resulting in a frustrating failure state21. The player is afforded no opportunity to recover, process their mastery, or experience a state of flow. According to Swink, optimal real-time control requires a continuous feedback loop where the game's response to an input occurs within a 50 to 200-millisecond perceptual cycle, allowing the player to formulate correction cycles34. As the game's speed increases linearly, the window for these correction cycles narrows to zero, breaking the illusion of control.  
A progressive, 3-tier endless run structure necessitates the implementation of a "sawtooth" difficulty curve. Instead of a continuous upward slope, the difficulty parameters should rise, peak, and then drop immediately upon transitioning into a new world tier20. This provides a vital pacing mechanism. When the player breaches the score threshold for World 2, the raw speed of the game (the JUMP\_DURATION\_RAMP and PLATFORM\_SPACING\_Z\_RAMP constants) should reset to a slightly lower baseline. However, to maintain the challenge, this numerical drop must be paired with the introduction of a new mechanical variable—specifically, the swaying platforms (Feature F4).  
By reducing the game speed while simultaneously introducing lateral platform movement, the game shifts the required player skill from pure twitch-reaction timing to spatial prediction and rhythm analysis. The cognitive load remains balanced; the player is not overwhelmed by extreme speed while trying to learn a new mechanic. As the run continues through World 2, the speed ramps back up, combining with the sway mechanic to push the difficulty to a new, higher peak before the World 3 transition.  
Mathematically, this can be achieved by decoupling the ramp modifiers from the absolute score and binding them to the *tier score* (the score accumulated since the start of the current world).

## **Size Constraints and Performance Benchmarks**

The hard constraint of delivering the entire overhaul within an additional \~20KB of source code, utilizing zero external assets and maintaining 60fps on mid-range mobile devices, is stringent but entirely realistic given modern WebGL programming paradigms.  
Benchmarks from size-constrained development competitions, most notably the JS13kGames competition, demonstrate that fully featured, procedural 3D games can be executed within a 13-kilobyte ZIP archive. Postmortems of JS13k entries, such as *Soul Jumper* and *Path to Glory*, reveal that complex procedural generation, WebGL rendering, and custom WebAudio synthesis can operate comfortably within extreme memory and storage limits when the architecture is rigorously optimized35.  
The success of this approach relies on aggressive code reuse and strict adherence to Three.js memory management protocols. The proposed world structure relies on a centralized configuration file (Worlds.ts). Because worlds are defined merely as data objects containing numerical overrides for existing parameters (e.g., color hex codes, ramp velocities), adding new worlds costs only a few hundred bytes of text, scaling infinitely without increasing the architectural footprint.  
The primary performance risk in procedural WebGL applications is the mismanagement of the GPU memory heap, leading to memory leaks and browser crashes. Three.js does not automatically garbage-collect GPU resources when an object is removed from the scene graph38. If a world transition involves instantiating new geometries, materials, or procedural textures, the previous assets will remain trapped in VRAM unless explicitly disposed of.  
To maintain the ![][image3] memory target established by the baseline game10, the cleanup sequence must be absolute. Upon transitioning between worlds, the system must traverse the outgoing scene entities and invoke geometry.dispose(), material.dispose(), and crucially, texture.dispose() on any canvas-generated backgrounds or gradients38. Failure to execute this exact disposal sequence will result in a linear accumulation of memory usage, eventually terminating the browser tab during longer sessions.  
Furthermore, rendering performance dictates how features are implemented. The baseline game utilizes individual THREE.Group objects for each platform10. While acceptable for a small number of visible steps, introducing moving platforms and particle bursts increases the CPU overhead required to calculate transformation matrices. As previously discussed, removing the black outline meshes neutralizes this overhead. Additionally, all stationary decorative elements (like the background rock clusters) should utilize THREE.InstancedMesh. Instancing allows the GPU to draw thousands of identical geometries using a single draw call, drastically reducing the CPU-bound driver validation bottleneck and guaranteeing a stable 60fps regardless of the environmental density38.

## **The Final Recommendation and Decision Matrix**

Based on the exhaustive analysis of art direction precedents, procedural rendering techniques, psychological pacing models, and WebGL performance constraints, the following overhaul configuration is recommended to maximize player retention per unit of developmental effort.

By repurposing the existing tri-planar halftone shader to render mathematical grids and utilizing additive blending for fake glow, the game achieves a premium aesthetic without the framerate destruction associated with organic noise generation or post-processing bloom.  
> 2. **World Structure: Phases in One Endless Run.** Mid-run transitions trigger a sawtooth difficulty curve, offering psychological rest phases that keep players in the flow state longer. This eliminates the massive retention friction associated with navigating map-select menus between failures.  
> 3. **Progression Model: Score Milestones.** Progressing through worlds based strictly on reaching numerical high-score thresholds requires zero new UI or persistent storage architecture. It leverages the "curiosity gap" to drive repeat sessions without the balancing nightmares of a dual-currency economy.

Feature F4 introduces lateral mechanical complexity that pairs perfectly with the speed reduction of the sawtooth difficulty curve. Feature F5 provides immediate, localized session goals to maintain micro-engagement.

## **Risks and Mitigations**

The implementation of this recommended strategy carries specific technical risks that must be proactively mitigated to maintain the integrity of the baseline architecture.  
**Garbage Collection (GC) Stutter During Transitions:** Dynamically generating new canvas textures (e.g., sky gradients) and instantiating new materials at the exact moment a player crosses a world threshold will invoke the browser's garbage collector. This triggers a main-thread pause, resulting in a severe frame drop (stutter) precisely when the player needs smooth control38.

* *Mitigation:* Pre-compute and instantiate all materials, fake glow sprites, and CanvasTexture sky gradients during the initial game boot sequence. Because the game targets a highly optimized \<50MB memory footprint, holding three world states in VRAM simultaneously is well within hardware limits. During a phase transition, the game logic should merely toggle visible flags or swap pre-existing reference pointers, ensuring a seamless, stutter-free visual shift.

**Physics Desynchronization on Moving Platforms:**  
The introduction of swaying platforms (Feature F4) fundamentally alters the collision detection paradigm. If the ball's interpolation (X\_LERP) to the center of the platform does not precisely account for the platform's lateral sine-wave offset at the exact frame of impact, the physics engine will register a "miss" even if the models appear to intersect visually.

* *Mitigation:* The swayOffset hook within the PlatformEntity must be deterministically calculated based on elapsed absolute time, entirely decoupled from variable render frame rates. The hit detection algorithm must evaluate the distance between the ball and the platform's *dynamically predicted* center at ![][image4], rather than its static origin.

## **Contradictions to the Brainstorm Baseline**

The deep research phase uncovered two critical contradictions regarding the assumptions made in the provided OVERHAUL\_BRAINSTORM.md document that require immediate architectural correction.  
**1\. Haptic Feedback is Non-Functional on iOS Safari:** The brainstorm document proposes Feature F7 (Haptics \+ flash), describing navigator.vibrate as a "negligible" code addition that provides "free mobile engagement." While the JavaScript footprint is indeed minuscule, empirical platform documentation explicitly confirms that the Web Vibration API (navigator.vibrate) is entirely unsupported on iOS Safari43. Given that iOS represents a massive segment of the mobile gaming market, relying on haptics to convey milestone achievements or physical impacts is a critical design failure.

* *Correction:* The game must rely entirely on visual and auditory "juice" to communicate impact. Developer talks from Vlambeer ("The Art of Screenshake") emphasize that GSAP-driven camera kick, hit-stop (briefly pausing the game time scale upon impact), and aggressive particle bursts must carry the full weight of the game's physical feedback, as hardware vibration cannot be guaranteed on web deployments46.

**2\. Removing Black Outlines is a Performance Mandate, Not Just an Aesthetic Choice:** The brainstorm frames the removal of black outlines as a stylistic pivot necessary to achieve the "layered depth" of a premium aesthetic. However, a structural analysis of the baseline architecture reveals that the outline is generated via the inverted hull method—duplicating every sphere and box geometry, scaling them by a factor of 1.05, and rendering them inside-out via a separate MeshBasicMaterial (eo(r,t) function)10. Removing this feature does not merely change the art style; it definitively halves the vertex count and draw call volume per frame.

* *Correction:* Removing the outlines must be prioritized as a fundamental performance optimization. Reclaiming 50% of the rendering budget is the exact technical maneuver that allows the game to afford the additive blending passes required for the Neon glow effects and the matrix calculations required for moving platforms, ensuring the 60fps target remains inviolate on lower-end devices.
